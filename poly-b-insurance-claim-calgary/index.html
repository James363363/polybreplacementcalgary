Poly-B Pipe Caused Water Damage? Here's How to Handle the Claim

Short answer: a Poly-B fitting that suddenly cracks or lets go is usually a covered event, but how you document it in the first hour can shape how smoothly the claim goes. Here's exactly what to do, what to keep, and what your insurer will likely ask for — in plain language, not insurance jargon.

A quick note before we go further: this page is general information for Calgary homeowners, not insurance or legal advice. Insurance policies, claims processes, and adjuster decisions vary by insurer, by policy, and by your individual circumstances. Always confirm coverage details and next steps directly with your own insurance broker or provider.

First: is this actually a covered claim?

Canadian home insurers draw a consistent line between two kinds of water damage. Sudden and accidental events — a fitting that cracks overnight, a pipe that bursts without warning — are typically treated as a covered peril under a standard homeowner policy. Gradual damage — a slow seep from a corroded or worn pipe that a homeowner should reasonably have caught and fixed — is usually excluded, because insurers treat that as a maintenance failure rather than an unforeseen event.

Poly-B sits in an interesting spot in that distinction. The material itself becomes brittle gradually, over years of exposure to chlorinated water — that part is a slow process nobody can see happening. But the actual failure, the moment a fitting cracks and water starts flowing, is almost always sudden. That's the event your policy is responding to, not the years of aging that led up to it. It's still worth understanding that some insurers or adjusters may ask more questions on a Poly-B claim than they would on a newer PEX system, simply because Poly-B's failure pattern is well documented in the industry.

What to do in the first hour

If water is actively running right now, stop reading and go shut off your main valve first — our emergency leak repair guide walks through that step by step. Once the water's off and things are stable, work through these steps in order to protect your claim.

  1. Photograph and video everything before you touch anything. Get wide shots of the affected room, close-ups of the source of the leak, and anything damaged — flooring, drywall, cabinets, belongings. Do this before you start mopping up or moving furniture.
  2. Keep the failed piece of pipe or fitting. Don't throw it in the trash with the rest of the mess. Bag it and set it aside — it's physical evidence of what failed, and it's often exactly what an adjuster or our plumbers will want to look at.
  3. Call your insurer as soon as you reasonably can. Most policies expect prompt notice of a loss. A quick call to start the claim doesn't commit you to anything, and it starts the clock on getting an adjuster assigned.
  4. Mitigate further damage, but don't over-clean. Move valuables and furniture off wet flooring, run a dehumidifier or fans if you have them, but avoid ripping out drywall or flooring yourself before your insurer has had a chance to see it or tell you what they need documented first.
  5. Keep every receipt from here forward. Emergency plumbing call-outs, hotel stays if you're displaced, cleanup supplies, water damage restoration — insurers typically want to see these as part of the claim.
  6. Call us for the plumbing side. Once the immediate danger is handled, (403) 555-0100 gets a plumber out to assess what failed and give you a written scope for repair or full replacement.

What your adjuster will likely ask about

Once a claim is open, expect some combination of the following. None of this means your claim is in trouble — it's standard information an insurer needs on any water damage file, and being ready with answers keeps things moving.

  • When the damage was first noticed, and by whom. This helps them place the event as sudden rather than something that had been building for a while.
  • Whether you've had any previous leaks or water damage claims in this home. A pattern of past Poly-B leaks doesn't automatically sink a new claim, but be upfront about it — insurers can usually find prior claims history anyway.
  • Photos, video, and the failed pipe or fitting itself. This is why keeping the evidence in steps one and two above matters so much.
  • A scope of the plumbing repair. Our written assessment of what failed and what it'll take to fix — whether that's a spot repair or a case for full replacement — usually becomes part of your claim file.
  • Whether the repair work is permitted. If the claim includes repiping and not just cosmetic repairs, a City of Calgary plumbing permit and a paid invoice from a licensed plumber is the documentation insurers, and later buyers or lenders, tend to want to see.

After the claim: fixing the plumbing itself

An insurance claim covers the damage a Poly-B failure caused — the flooring, the drywall, the cabinets. It generally doesn't pay to proactively replace the rest of your Poly-B system, even after one section has failed. That's a separate decision, and it's worth making deliberately rather than in the middle of a stressful claim.

What we see most often: a homeowner gets one section patched or repaired as part of settling the claim, then within a year or two calls about a second leak somewhere else in the same house. That's because the rest of the Poly-B installed at the same time has been aging under the same conditions. If you've just been through one claim, it's a reasonable moment to look at the full picture — see our replacement cost guide for realistic Calgary pricing, and our home insurance and Poly-B guide for how a completed repipe can affect your premium and future coverage.

Frequently asked questions

Is water damage from a Poly-B pipe automatically covered by insurance?

Not automatically, but it's usually a good starting position. Canadian home insurers generally cover water damage from a pipe failure that's sudden and accidental — a fitting that cracks and floods a room overnight, for example. What's typically excluded is damage from a slow, ongoing leak that a homeowner should have caught and fixed. Poly-B pipe becomes brittle gradually over years, but the actual moment it lets go is usually sudden, which is the distinction that matters for coverage. Every policy words this differently, so confirm with your broker rather than assuming.

What if my insurer calls it a "gradual" leak instead of a sudden failure?

This is worth understanding before it comes up, not after. Insurers draw a real line between a pipe that suddenly bursts (usually covered) and a slow seep from a corroded or worn-out pipe that a homeowner arguably should have maintained (usually not covered). Since Poly-B is a well-known aging material, don't be surprised if an adjuster asks pointed questions about how long the area was damp or whether you'd noticed earlier signs. Answer honestly, but also push back with the facts of your specific situation — a fitting that failed suddenly is a different event than a leak you'd been ignoring for months, even if both involve the same material.

Should I keep the broken piece of pipe, or throw it out?

Keep it. Insurers treat damaged materials as evidence, and a cracked fitting or split section of Poly-B is exactly the kind of thing your adjuster may want to see, or that we can point to when we explain what failed and why. Bag it, keep it dry, and don't discard it until your claim is settled and your repipe is done.

Do I need a permit before my insurance claim will be paid?

The claim for the water damage itself (flooring, drywall, cabinets) doesn't usually hinge on a permit. But if the claim also involves fixing or replacing the failed plumbing, insurers increasingly want to see that the repair work itself was done properly — a City of Calgary plumbing permit and a paid invoice from a licensed plumber is the paperwork that shows the job was inspected and done to code, and it's the same documentation you'll want on file for a future sale or a premium adjustment either way.

Will filing a claim raise my premium or get my policy cancelled?

It can, but it isn't automatic, and it varies a lot by insurer and by your claims history overall. A single water damage claim is common enough that most insurers won't cancel a policy over it, though a premium increase at renewal is possible, especially if you've had more than one water-related claim. This is another one worth asking your broker about directly rather than guessing, since the answer depends on your specific insurer's underwriting rules.
