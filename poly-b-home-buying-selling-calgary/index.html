Inspection Just Flagged Poly-B? Here's What It Means For Your Deal

Whether you're a buyer staring at a home inspection report or a seller wondering if you need to deal with your Poly-B pipe before you list, you're not the first Calgarian to hit this exact moment. Here's how it usually plays out on both sides of the transaction — and where a plumber's opinion can help before you negotiate.

This page comes up a lot for two kinds of Calgary homeowners: a buyer whose inspector just wrote "polybutylene" on page 12 of the report, and a seller who's about to list a 1978–1995-built home and wants to know if Poly-B is going to become a headache in negotiations. Both situations have the same starting point — knowing what you're actually dealing with before anyone signs anything.

For Buyers: What a Poly-B Flag Actually Means

  • It's a risk flag, not necessarily an active problem. Most Poly-B systems in Calgary are still functioning day to day — the concern is that the pipe is known to fail more often over time, especially at fittings, which means it's a future cost and insurance/financing consideration rather than an emergency by default.
  • It's a legitimate negotiating point. Buyers commonly use a Poly-B finding to ask for one of three things: a price reduction, a seller credit toward replacement after closing, or a condition that the seller replace the plumbing before the deal closes. Which one makes sense depends on your timeline and how competitive the offer needs to be.
  • Financing and insurance can be affected. Some mortgage lenders and home insurers ask about plumbing material during underwriting, and a few attach conditions to Poly-B homes. It's worth flagging this early with your mortgage broker and insurance broker — not just your realtor — so it doesn't surface as a last-minute surprise close to your financing deadline. See our Poly-B and home insurance guide for more on how that conversation usually goes.
  • Get an independent plumber's quote before you negotiate. A home inspector's report tells you the pipe is there; it doesn't tell you what replacement will actually cost for that specific house. A written quote gives you a real number to negotiate with instead of a guess, and it's evidence you can bring to the table.

For Sellers: Replace First, or Price/Disclose As-Is?

  • Replacing proactively costs money upfront but simplifies everything after. A completed repipe before listing removes Poly-B from the conversation entirely, can widen your buyer pool to people who'd otherwise pass on an older system, and avoids the back-and-forth negotiation that tends to happen once an inspector flags it mid-transaction.
  • Pricing or disclosing as-is is honest and very common in Calgary. Plenty of homes with Poly-B sell every year without a replacement — sellers price it in, disclose it upfront, or let a buyer's inspection surface it. Just expect it to come up in almost every serious offer once a buyer's inspector spots it, so it's worth being ready for that conversation rather than hoping it doesn't happen.
  • Hoping an inspector misses it isn't a strategy. Poly-B is easy for a competent inspector to identify, and in a private or non-represented sale especially, being upfront about known plumbing issues is the safer path — both for the transaction going smoothly and for your own peace of mind after closing.
  • A documented, permitted, warrantied replacement can be a selling point. If you do replace before listing, keep the permit, the invoice, and any warranty paperwork. "Fully re-piped with PEX, permitted work, warrantied" is a line that stands out in a listing next to comparable homes that still say "original plumbing."

A Note on Disclosure — This Isn't Legal Advice

Alberta's real estate disclosure rules, and what a seller is specifically required to put in writing about known plumbing issues, are more detailed than general guides like this one can responsibly cover. This page is meant to give you the practical, plumbing-side picture — not to tell you exactly what your disclosure statement should say. Before you list or make an offer, confirm the specifics with your own realtor or a real estate lawyer, who can speak to your exact contract, property, and situation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to disclose Poly-B when selling my Calgary home?

In general, Alberta sellers are expected to be honest about known material defects, and Poly-B you're aware of would typically be part of that conversation. But exactly what you're required to disclose, and how it should be worded, depends on your specific situation. Confirm your exact obligations with your realtor or a real estate lawyer before you list — this page is general information, not legal advice.

Can a home with Poly-B still get a mortgage?

Usually, yes — most Poly-B homes in Calgary buy and sell without the financing falling through. That said, some lenders and insurers pay closer attention to older plumbing and may attach conditions or ask for documentation. Flag it with your mortgage broker and insurance broker early in the process so it doesn't become a last-minute problem before closing.

Should I replace before listing, or offer a credit instead?

Both approaches are common here. Replacing first removes the issue from every negotiation and can attract more buyers, but it costs money upfront and takes time to schedule and complete. Offering a credit keeps cash in your pocket now but means you should expect the topic to come up in most serious offers. Which makes more sense depends on your timeline, budget, and how the market is moving — worth a conversation with your realtor.

Does replacing Poly-B increase my home's resale value?

It's not usually a dollar-for-dollar bump, but a documented, permitted, warrantied PEX repipe removes a common buyer objection and can make your home stand out next to similar listings that still have original plumbing. For many buyers, one less thing to worry about is genuinely worth something.

What does it mean if a home inspector flags Poly-B in my inspection report?

Usually it just means the inspector correctly identified the pipe material and is flagging it as a known risk factor for the future — not that something is actively wrong right now. It's useful information you can act on: negotiate with it, plan a replacement timeline around it, or get a second opinion from a plumber before deciding what to do next.
