Two Different Eras, Two Different Materials

Galvanized steel pipe became the standard water supply material in North American homes around the 1940s and stayed common through the mid-20th century — homes built before roughly 1970 are the ones most likely to have it. Poly-B came later, commonly installed in Calgary homes built between about 1978 and 1995, before it was phased out. If your home falls outside that 1978–1995 window, especially if it's significantly older, galvanized is at least as likely a guess as Poly-B — and it's worth confirming which one you actually have before assuming.

How to Tell Them Apart

Bend it

Poly-B is flexible plastic that bends by hand. Galvanized is rigid steel — it won't flex, and it comes in straight sections, not coils.

Try a magnet

Galvanized is steel, so a magnet sticks to it. Poly-B is plastic, so a magnet won't grab it at all.

Look at the joints

Galvanized sections screw together with threaded metal fittings. Poly-B uses crimp rings and plastic fittings — no threads.

Colour is a rougher clue but still useful: galvanized typically looks dull silver-grey metal, sometimes with rust-coloured staining at the joints over time, while Poly-B is a dull grey, black, or cream-coloured plastic. If you're genuinely unsure, our Poly-B warning signs guide covers what Poly-B specifically looks like in more detail, including the printed PB2110 stamp that confirms it beyond doubt.

Why They Fail Completely Differently

Galvanized pipe fails through corrosion. The zinc coating that protects the steel wears away over time, and rust builds up on the inside of the pipe — this narrows the internal opening, which is why homes with galvanized supply lines often notice gradually weakening water pressure years before an actual leak shows up. Eventually corrosion can eat all the way through the pipe wall, causing a pinhole leak, or discolour your water a rusty orange or brown. A typical lifespan is often cited around 20 years, with significant deterioration common by 30 to 40.

Poly-B doesn't rust — it's plastic, so internal corrosion and the pressure loss that comes with it simply aren't part of its failure pattern. Instead, as covered in our warning signs guide, chlorine exposure in tap water gradually makes the plastic and its fittings brittle, and the fittings tend to be the actual point of failure — cracking or letting go at a joint, sometimes without the gradual pressure-loss warning that galvanized gives you. Different material, different mechanism, different warning signs to watch for.

What Either One Means for Your Home

Both materials are ones plumbers, home inspectors, and insurers treat as aging plumbing that eventually needs replacing — see our home inspection guide for how an inspector documents an older material like either of these on a report. If your home has galvanized pipe with declining water pressure, or Poly-B with any of the warning signs above, the practical fix is largely the same either way: a full repipe with modern PEX, rather than chasing individual patches on a system that's failing throughout, not just at one spot.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if my pipe is Poly-B or galvanized steel?

Look at the pipe itself. Galvanized pipe is rigid, heavy, and metallic — straight sections joined with threaded fittings, and a magnet will stick to it since it's steel. Poly-B is flexible plastic, usually dull grey, black, or cream, bends by hand, and is joined with crimp rings and plastic fittings rather than threads. If you can bend it or a magnet won't stick, it's not galvanized.

Could my Calgary home have both galvanized and Poly-B pipe?

Yes, especially if it's had partial plumbing updates over the decades. It's common to find original galvanized supply lines with a section replaced in Poly-B at some point, or Poly-B added for an addition or renovation while older galvanized runs stayed in place elsewhere. The only way to know for certain what's throughout the whole house is to have a plumber check the areas you can't see.

Which is worse, Poly-B or galvanized steel pipe?

They're both materials plumbers recommend replacing, but they fail in different ways rather than one being straightforwardly worse. Galvanized corrodes from the inside, gradually choking off water flow and eventually developing pinhole leaks. Poly-B doesn't rust, but its plastic and fittings become brittle over time and can crack or fail at a joint, sometimes without warning. Age and condition matter more than which material you happen to have.

Does galvanized pipe affect home insurance the same way Poly-B does?

Both are considered older plumbing materials that insurers and home inspectors pay closer attention to, though the specific underwriting treatment varies by insurer and isn't identical for the two materials. If your home has either one, it's worth asking your insurance broker directly how they treat it, the same way you would for Poly-B.

Can galvanized pipe just be repaired instead of replaced?

A single section can sometimes be patched or replaced, but since the corrosion causing reduced water pressure is happening throughout the whole system, a patch on one spot doesn't fix flow problems elsewhere in the house. Most plumbers treat galvanized the same way they treat Poly-B in an older home: a full repipe is usually the real fix, not a series of spot repairs.
