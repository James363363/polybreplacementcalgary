DIY vs. Hiring a Plumber

Can You Replace Your Own Poly-B Pipe? Here's the Honest Answer.

If you're handy and pricing out a Poly-B repipe, DIY is a reasonable question to ask. Here's what's actually legal, what the real risks are once the wall closes back up, and why most Calgary homeowners land on hiring a licensed plumber even when they could technically do some of it themselves.

The Short Answer

Legally, yes — but only in a narrow way. Homeowners can self-permit plumbing work if they own and occupy the home and are doing the work themselves, meaning you pull the City of Calgary permit in your own name and go through the same rough-in and final inspections a licensed contractor would (see our permit requirements guide for the full process). What you can't do is have an unlicensed friend or handyman do the work for you and call it DIY — the self-permit path only covers work you personally perform, and plumbing is a compulsory certification trade in Alberta for anyone doing it professionally.

Practically, most Calgary homeowners who look closely at what a full repipe actually involves decide it's not worth doing themselves. Here's why.

What Makes This a Harder DIY Than It Looks

Concealed, not visible, work

Most of a repipe happens behind drywall or ceilings — a mistake doesn't show up until it's already closed back in and hard to reach.

Specialized tools per connection

Proper PEX crimping needs the right crimp tool and go/no-go gauge for every fitting size — not a generic pair of pliers.

Whole-house scope

This isn't one fitting — it's every water line in the house, run zone by zone, each connection needing to be right.

None of this means it's impossible for a genuinely skilled DIYer with the right tools. It means the margin for a small mistake to become an expensive, hidden problem is much wider than most first-time DIY plumbing jobs.

What Actually Goes Wrong

The most common DIY repipe failure isn't a dramatic burst pipe on day one — it's a crimp connection that wasn't seated quite right, which holds fine for weeks or months before it lets go slowly behind a wall. By the time you notice a stain or a musty smell, water's been getting into your framing and insulation for a while. A licensed plumber has made this exact connection hundreds of times and knows what correct actually feels like; a first-time DIYer is learning that on the job, with the feedback loop being water damage in your own home rather than a supervisor catching it.

There's also the permit and inspection side. Skipping the rough-in inspection — even on a self-permitted job — means no one verified the work before the wall went back up, which can mean redoing finished work later, and can complicate an insurance claim if a leak happens down the road and the insurer finds unpermitted or uninspected plumbing behind it.

The Real Cost-Benefit

DIY saves you labour cost, which is real money on a job this size. But it doesn't save you the cost of materials, and it adds costs that are easy to underestimate: a proper crimping tool and gauge set, a pressure-test setup, and enough PEX, fittings, and shutoff valves to run an entire house. Add the hours of learning curve on a job most people do exactly once, and the gap between DIY and hiring a pro is often smaller than it looks on paper — especially if anything goes wrong and you end up paying a licensed plumber to fix a DIY mistake on top of the original job. Where DIY Actually Makes Sense

A full concealed repipe is a different category of job from a single visible, accessible fitting — say, one crimp connection under a kitchen sink that you can see, test, and fix on the spot if something's wrong. That kind of small, visible, easily-inspected job is a far lower-stakes place to build confidence than starting with a whole-house repipe behind walls and ceilings. Even there, it's worth confirming with the city whether that specific piece of work needs its own permit before you start. Frequently Asked Questions

Is it actually legal to replace my own Poly-B pipe in Calgary?

In narrow circumstances, yes. Homeowners may self-permit plumbing work only if they own and occupy the home and are doing the work themselves — you still need to pull the City of Calgary plumbing permit yourself and pass the same rough-in and final inspections a licensed contractor would. You can't have someone unlicensed do the work for you and call it DIY; the self-permit option only covers work you personally perform.

What's the biggest risk of doing a repipe yourself?

A crimp connection that isn't seated correctly won't necessarily leak right away — it can hold for weeks or months before it fails behind a wall you've already closed back up. A licensed plumber has done hundreds of these connections and knows what a correctly seated crimp feels and looks like; a first-timer is learning that on your own walls, with your own water damage as the feedback if something's wrong.

Will unpermitted DIY plumbing work affect my home insurance?

It can. If a leak happens later and an insurer discovers the work wasn't permitted or inspected, that can complicate or reduce a claim, since insurers generally expect plumbing work to meet code. A permitted, inspected job — whether DIY or contractor-done — gives you a paper trail showing the work was done correctly, which is exactly what an adjuster wants to see.

Does DIY actually save meaningful money on a full repipe?

It saves on labour, but a full repipe involves specialized crimping tools, a pressure-test setup, and enough PEX and fittings to run an entire house — costs that add up fast for a one-time job. Add the time investment of learning the work correctly, and the savings are often smaller than they look on paper, especially if a mistake means paying a licensed plumber to fix it afterward anyway.

Is there any part of a Poly-B repipe that's reasonable to DIY?

Small, visible, easily-inspected jobs — like swapping a single accessible fitting under a sink — are a much lower-stakes place to start than a full concealed repipe through walls and ceilings. Even then, it's worth confirming with the city whether that specific work needs a permit before you start.
