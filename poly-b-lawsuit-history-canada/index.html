Can You Still Get Compensation for Poly-B Pipes?

If you've searched "Poly-B lawsuit" or "Poly-B class action," you're probably hoping the answer is yes — that somewhere out there is a fund that will pay to replace the pipe in your home. It's a fair thing to hope for. Unfortunately, for the vast majority of Calgary homeowners today, the honest answer is no. Poly-B was the subject of well-known class action litigation in Canada and the United States back in the 1990s, but those claims programs had defined filing deadlines, and those deadlines passed long ago — by the early-to-mid 2000s. There's no active fund a homeowner can apply to in 2026.

That means if your home still has Poly-B piping, the practical reality is that replacement is a cost you plan and budget for yourself, much like a roof or a furnace — not a repair a manufacturer settlement covers. The good news is that once you know that going in, you can make smart, proactive decisions instead of waiting on a legal outcome that isn't coming. A Quick, General History of Poly-B in North America

Understanding the timeline helps explain why things landed where they did. Here's the broad strokes, in general historical terms:

  1. Late 1970s – introduction. Polybutylene ("Poly-B") pipe was introduced across North America as a cheaper, faster-to-install alternative to copper. Builders and developers embraced it quickly for both new construction and renovations, including a large number of Calgary homes built through the late '70s, '80s, and into the mid-'90s.
  2. 1980s–early 1990s – widespread installation. Poly-B became common in single-family homes, townhouses, and condos across the city. At the time, it was seen as a modern, cost-effective plumbing choice.
  3. 1990s – problems emerge. Over time, homeowners and plumbers began reporting brittle pipe walls and failures — particularly at the plastic and acetal crimp fittings used to join sections of pipe. These failures often showed up as slow leaks inside walls or ceilings long before anything was visible.
  4. 1990s – class action litigation. As failure reports grew, major class action lawsuits were filed in both Canada and the United States against manufacturers and parties involved in the piping systems. Those legal actions led to compensation programs with their own defined claim-filing windows.
  5. Mid-to-late 1990s – builders move on. Confidence in the product dropped industry-wide, and builders largely stopped installing Poly-B in favour of PEX and copper by the mid-to-late 1990s.
  6. Today. The claims windows from that 1990s litigation are closed. Homes with existing Poly-B are still common across Calgary, and replacement today is handled privately, house by house — which is exactly where a company like ours comes in.

Why You Can't Just "File a Claim" Today

It's a reasonable question: if there was a lawsuit, why can't a homeowner just submit their address and get paid? The reason is that those historical claims programs weren't open-ended — they had fixed deadlines set when the litigation was settled, and homeowners needed to file within that window to be eligible. Once a deadline passes, it passes for everyone, regardless of when their pipe actually fails. Since those windows closed years ago, there is no live process today for a Calgary homeowner to submit a new claim tied to that original litigation.

This is actually the most useful thing to take away from the whole Poly-B legal history: waiting on legal recourse is not a viable plan anymore. The practical path is proactive planning — understanding your home's plumbing, having an honest conversation with your home insurance provider, and budgeting for replacement before a fitting fails rather than after. Was Poly-B "Recalled"? Setting the Record Straight

Not in the way a car or a food product gets recalled. There was no government order requiring every Calgary home to have its Poly-B pipe removed. What happened instead was a slower, real-world process: failure rates and durability concerns showed up over years of use, that pattern fed into the class action litigation described above, and the building industry and insurance sector responded by moving away from the product and, in some cases, factoring it into underwriting decisions. It's more accurate to think of Poly-B as a piping material that fell out of favour due to documented reliability problems than as a product that was formally recalled.

  • Knowing the real history means you can stop waiting on a settlement and start budgeting realistically, so a surprise pipe failure never turns into a surprise bill you weren't prepared for.
  • Understanding it's not a mandatory recall means you're free to plan replacement on your own timeline — proactively before a leak, rather than being forced into an emergency.
  • Knowing insurers pay attention to Poly-B means getting ahead of it can protect your premiums and your coverage, not just your walls. See our insurance guide for specifics.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I still file a claim for my Poly-B pipes?

Generally, no. Those old class action claims windows closed years ago, back in the early-to-mid 2000s. Most Calgary homeowners today are simply too late to file under the original litigation. If you think your situation is unusual — maybe a builder-specific warranty, or something outside the standard timeline — that's a conversation for a licensed lawyer, not something we can advise on.

Was Poly-B recalled?

Not officially, no. There was never a government-mandated recall forcing removal. It's more that failure reports built up through the 1990s, lawsuits followed, and the industry quietly moved on to PEX and copper. Your home isn't "in violation" of anything for still having it.

Why did builders stop using Poly-B?

Mostly confidence — once brittle pipes and failed fittings started showing up across enough homes, and the lawsuits followed, builders didn't want the liability or the callbacks. By the mid-to-late '90s it was pretty much gone from new construction in favour of PEX.

Is having Poly-B pipe illegal or against code today?

No, not at all. Plenty of Calgary homes built between 1978 and 1995 still have it, and that's completely legal. It's just not used in new work anymore. Whether to replace it is entirely your call — many owners do it proactively to lower leak risk rather than wait for a failure.

Will my insurance company pay to replace my Poly-B pipes?

Usually not before a leak happens — insurance tends to cover the damage after a failure, not the preventive cost of swapping the pipe out. Some insurers offer a premium break for replacing it, or require it after a claim. Worth a call to your provider to find out where you stand.
